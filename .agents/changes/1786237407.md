# The takes

> Change `1786237407` — 2026-08-08

## Goal

Nine settings' values were never read by the preview at all — `transitionMode`, `windowAnimation`,
`coverMode`, `holdTimeout`, `trackpadScroll`, `trackpadScrollDirection`, `resizeDetent`,
`focusFollowsMouse` and a height selection — so changing any of them moved nothing. Several more showed
the wrong thing: `height-presets` animated a **width**; `focus.follows-mouse` played its own inverse,
with focus moving and the pointer chasing it; `mouse.follows-focus` played that same take, so two
opposite settings were one picture; `mouse.hide` meant "never draw a cursor" rather than "take one
away"; `center-focused-column` played over a set that fitted on one screen, where centring and the
minimal reveal are the same arrangement; the Guide tab opened on a desktop with no guide on it. And a
row with no take fell back to its *section's* take, so crossing `interactive-resize` or `hold-timeout`
on the way down the panel cut the stage away from whatever was playing.

The rest had a picture that could not be read. A gap is a number in points and was shown at whatever
size a whole desktop happens to be; the four outer-gap fields had nothing at all, since the value they
move is a margin nobody had drawn. `layout.interactive-resize` was called "Adopt a hand resize" in a
window whose whole job is to explain the file's own vocabulary.

The design was a house style — a handful of rules every take obeys — plus a choreography per setting. The
rules that ended up load-bearing in code are named in §7 of `IMPLEMENTATION.md`.

## Implementation

A capability at a time, each finishing its settings before the next began.

**`MockContent`.** A pane is one image: the whole window interior — title band, stoplights, the app's
icon and a suggestion of the app — drawn in Core Graphics from a short vocabulary of furniture, at
true-point sizes rasterized at mock scale. It earns its place three times: a pane stops reading as an
icon on a slab, the guide can borrow the same still for `guide.style = preview`, and a still is an image
a rect can stretch or crop, which *is* `animation.window`. The still is taken **on arrival and never in
flight** — mid-motion the pane paints the picture it had before the move into a rect that no longer fits
it, which is what a compositor does with a screenshot. `PaneLayer` lost a dozen sublayers to it.

**The camera.** `Projection.camera` is a rect inside the monitor's clip that `mock(_:)` maps through, so
one number carries a push-in through a title bar, a corner radius and a shadow together and the bezel
never moves. `Camera.frame(containing:display:…)` only ever *grows* a subject to the display's aspect —
legibility bought with the camera and never with a lie — so a full-height column lands on very nearly the
whole display, and that is the honest answer rather than a shortcoming. It is a property of the
**setting**, so crossing from `column-gap` to `window-gap` is a pan.

**Life size is the ceiling, as arithmetic.** `Camera.maximumScale` bounds every framing at one mock point
per real point, which is "nothing is drawn larger than it is" stated so that it can be checked rather
than remembered. A gap is a length judged by eye — that *is* the demonstration — so 8, 12, 40 and 120 pt
all measure exactly themselves on the slab. The floor is `Projection.lifeSizeWidth`, and it only ever
widens the frame, so containment still outranks it and no subject can be lost to it. The one framing
allowed closer is the guide's own panel, at 1.6×, because nothing read there is a length: `guide.style`
is what a tile draws and `guide.span` is how many there are. `guide.gap` is points, and it is framed by
`guideCorner`, which keeps the cap.

**What a gap is measured against.** Not the two windows but the **two edges they hold apart**, which is
what lets both gap settings be read at a push-in instead of one of them resting at wide: `seams(slack:)`
takes the focused column and a quarter of its width either side at one window's height, and `stackSeam`
takes one window's height centred on the seam *inside* the column. Same shot, a right angle apart, so
crossing the two rows turns the lens rather than changing the subject.

**Rejected: a ~1.6× resting frame for the pointer settings.** On a tiled desktop every window is full
height, so a 1.6× push-in crops the very windows the cursor's position is measured against. Containing
the subject whole outranks the zoom, and the cursor is legible at wide (a 20 pt arrow is ~10 mock points
at `k = 0.52`). `Camera.closer` was written, tried, and deleted; `focus.follows-mouse` also needs the
screen's right edge for its reveal clause.

**The two things the window says about the desktop.** The **ring** is a claim that focus is part of what
a setting is about, so `Take.showsFocus` turns it off for the six Layout settings that are pure geometry:
a gap is the same number whichever window is focused, and a blue border on one of them is a subject the
setting does not have for the eye to follow. The **mark** is `Mark.Drawn`, and there are two — the gutter
an outer gap holds open, and the flush tick, granted only where a column edge really has landed on a
viewport edge. A gutter is drawn as the band itself, the whole length of its own side, washed inside a
1 pt accent outline: the outline says which edge and where content starts, the wash says how much. At `0`
the rect is a point thick and the border fills it, so a gap of nothing is an accent hairline with no
special case anywhere.

**The wash is measured, and that is not a second accent.** It lies on the user's own desktop picture,
which can be any colour at all, so `Wallpaper.luminance(of:layer:)` samples the strip the band covers and
flips it black or white — the same question the mock menu bar asks of the strip under itself, and the
same one macOS asks. Sampled through the *wallpaper layer* rather than the slab, since under a push-in
that layer is larger than the clip and slid sideways; a band thinner than `inkSampleDepth` asks about the
ground it sits on, because a hairline is thinner than a pixel of a desktop picture. Cached on the rect,
or a `CGImage` crop and a downscale would be the price of moving a lens.

**The pointer as an actor.** A scripted cursor is `PreviewModel`'s from end to end, which is what lets
focus be read off where the cursor *is* this frame, so the ring transfers on the seam crossing rather
than on the beat that started the move (`PointerActorTests` asserts they are the same sampled frame).
`PreviewMotion` has no pointer spring: a warp is a jump, because `CGWarpMouseCursorPosition` is one, and
a hand traces its own path. That path is a bowed arc walked under `PreviewModel.reach`, the minimum-jerk
profile a person reaching for something traces — at a constant speed a cursor sets off and stops dead
inside one frame, and what that reads as is a teleport followed by a slide. The bow is capped at 18 pt,
because the swing belongs to the wrist rather than to the distance, and a tenth of a long travel turns a
line into a hop. **"A hand is not a spring" is about the hand as an input**: `scrub` and `dragEdge` still
track it 1:1 with nothing in between, and must.

**A warp is an event.** `mouse.follows-focus` reaches the preview through the reducer's own two
predicates — `warps(pointerCaused:)` and `recentres` — asked where focus has just moved, and the pointer
parks there. Asked every frame instead it stops being that setting and becomes "the cursor is always in
the middle of the focused window", which pins the hand through every take that carries one and holds a
cursor at a window's centre while a script walks it to that window's edge. The fold takes the same
correction: focus answering a hover is folded at every beat rather than only at the end, because a
command back to the window a hover already left is a focus change, and one the fold cannot see is a
pointer that is never sent after it. `mouse.follows-focus`'s own take then has to walk the hand back into
the window before B, whose whole content is focus landing on the window the pointer is *already inside* —
a premise to establish rather than assume.

**The cue, and floats.** A badge at the low centre with **taken** and **declined**. It is drawn at the
settings window's scale rather than the desktop's, which is not a violation of "nothing is drawn larger
than it is": a cue is not on the desktop, it is the window saying what the hands did. It names the cause
as the **command** — `focus right`, monospaced, the exact text a `[keys]` binding takes — rather than as
a chord: a binding is one user's, and `⌥⇧L` is unreadable to anyone who has not bound it. `⌘⇥` lives on
in exactly one take, because `focus.system-events` is about focus emira did not cause and there is no
command to name; the badge being a foreign chord there is the distinction rather than an inconsistency.
The spelling is a `String` since the fence keeps `Command` out of the module, and `CueTests` parses each
one back and checks it re-emits unchanged. That setting also needed a set carrying three kinds of target,
so `Scene` gained floats — and the `admits` ladder is the reducer's own, derived from the geometry rather
than from a flag on the beat.

**Hand-driven travel.** `Beat.scrub`/`lift` give a scroll offset independent of focus framing, and
`PreviewState.travel` tells the view whether a hand is on it (draw exactly this), a lift is coasting (the
glide spring) or neither. The flush tick is granted only where a column edge really has landed on the
viewport edge the motion was heading toward — asking about "any coincidence" answers yes at rest.

**The detent stages its own distance.** `Scenes.detentPair` is the one set on the panel that spends
explicit `widthOverride`s, and it has to: a detent is a *distance to the screen's edge*, which off a
user's own ladder is anything at all — already zero where two presets fill the screen, so the first press
is the catch and there is nothing to compare it against. An override is also what a `grow` leaves behind,
so a take about continuous widths starting from one is the object it is about to produce, and
`Beat.widthOverride` is what returns the loop to it. Two presses of `grow 30%`: the first takes its whole
delta, the second arrives at a quarter of it and stops with the strip flush against the screen — or,
unchecked, carries the neighbour a fifth of a screen off it.

**Rejected: a third press.** It would show the help sentence's other clause, that a press made from the
notch passes through it, but at this step size both rungs reach the screen's width and clamp there, so
the loop would end on one picture for two settings. Three presses at 20% keep the clause and give up the
ending; the ending is where the eye rests.

**The drag.** During an edge drag the layout is deliberately untouched, so the neighbours hold still and
the dragged window grows *over* one of them; that needed `PreviewState.raised`, or the neighbour paints
over the very edge the hand is carrying. The dragged column is the **narrow** one, so the neighbour it
grows over is still visible standing still — which is the whole point of the neighbour not moving. The
circuit is a line rather than a triangle: the hand rests *on* the handle, pulls the edge out, lets go,
and walks back to where the edge now is, so there is one thing to watch and it is at one height. The
rewind rides that walk, since under `on` the width really is adopted and the rung has to be restored for
the loop to start over. A released hand **stays where it was let go**, read before the adoption so both
rungs answer the same point: under `off` the width is taken back, and a cursor still pinned to the
window's edge is dragged home with it — a hand teleporting rather than a layout changing its mind.
`Beat.dragEdge` carries a `SizeDelta` for `grow`'s reason, since a hand crossing a third of the screen is
a third of *any* screen where 90 pt is a gesture that shrinks as the display grows. The lens stays wide
throughout: a lens travelling while the thing it frames changes size is two motions at once.
`interactive-resize` and `animation.cover` came off `notDemonstrable`, which is now one entry.

**Arrival timing.** `animation.transition` governs every take rather than only its own, the same
discipline the springs keep. `snap` is `PreviewMotion.snap`; `off` holds each window at its old frame for
a fixed stagger, smallest first, and then jumps — no springs anywhere, because what is being shown is
latency.

**Spring pacing.** `Take.paced(by:)` respaces a script so one motion completes and then rests, with the
interval solved from the spring being edited: `(1 + u)e^(−u) = ε/D` by bisection, §8 of
`IMPLEMENTATION.md`'s arithmetic read the other way. `movement` got a `move-window` (pure translation,
so it is the only spring in the shot) and `glide` got the coast after a lift.

**The guide.** Eight columns, three screens — a shorter set makes `width` and `span` read as broken. The
guide is **raised by motion and lowered by `duration`**, so its whole life cycle is on screen every few
seconds and `off` genuinely means nothing appears; the period is derived from the value. The panel
travels between corners on a `RectAnimator` under the movement spring. `GuidePreview.Tile` carries a
`WindowId` so `preview` can borrow the pane's own still, which is the relationship the real guide has
with the real cover.

## Observations

- **`animation.cover`'s `immediate` sharpens on arrival rather than ~200 ms in.** The still is re-taken
  when a window lands, so the stale picture is stretched for exactly as long as the motion lasts. That is
  a difference in timing, not in kind — both rungs still show their own shape, a pause against a soft
  window. Tightening it would mean a second capture clock inside `PaneLayer`.
- **`animation.window`'s grow runs at the user's own resize spring** rather than a fixed 1.2 s each
  way. The take spaces its beats 1.2 s apart, but how long the stretch is *visible* is the spring's, and
  imposing a duration would be the preview disagreeing with the number beside it.
- **The cover take's rearrangement carries a width change.** A stale still can only be *seen* to be stale
  where the rect it is painted into has changed shape, and the mock's content does not change over time —
  so a pure `move-window` paints the same picture into the same size under both rungs.
- **A still is rasterized at the resting scale, so a pushed-in camera upsamples it.** `MockContent.still`
  keys its cache on `k` rather than on the framing, which is what stops a pan from redrawing every pane
  every frame — but the two gap framings rest at life size, nearly twice `k`, and there the panes are
  softer than the chrome around them. Capturing at the take's *resting* camera scale and re-capturing
  when the lens settles would fix it without reintroducing the thrash.
- **The four edges of one wallpaper can ink differently**, and on a photograph they do: a bright sky at
  the top and a dark ridge at the bottom put opposite washes on those two bands. Only one is ever on
  screen at a time, and each is right about its own ground.
- **The flush tick is a point wide against the monitor's own border**, which is where a right-edge catch
  lands it. It reads as confirmation once you know to look rather than as the announcement; what carries
  the detent is 30% against 8% and a neighbour that stays on screen. Widening it would have to answer for
  the magnet take, which shares it.
- `layout.outer-gap`'s own row — the one the four cells sit on — marks nothing, so the label reports a key
  with no picture while each cell has one. A `Mark` case for all four bands would fix it.
- `Take` has seven fields and `Beat` twenty-two cases. Both are still one switch each and the compiler
  checks them, but the next capability should probably fold rather than add.
- The visual pass was done twice over, and both are worth keeping. Rendering `DesktopView` offscreen to
  PNGs from a scratch test is deterministic and does not take the screen — but `CALayer.render(in:)`
  ignores `zPosition` and `isHidden`, so the sheet has to sort and filter the sublayers itself or it
  shows a different stacking than the screen does. Driving the real view and letting `screencapture` take
  the pixels off the panel is what a measured wash has to be checked against, since it is the only way to
  see it over the actual desktop picture. Pacing was checked by plotting per-frame cursor travel at
  60 Hz: a bell curve leaving at single-digit pt/s is a hand, a flat line is a tween.
