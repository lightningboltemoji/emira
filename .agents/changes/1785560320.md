# The guide

> Change `1785560320` — 2026-07-31

## Goal

The strip is infinite and the screen is not, so a user who scrolls two columns has no way to tell how much strip is
behind them, how much is ahead, or where the window they just focused sits in the whole. The cover answers that
question for the half-second it is up and then takes the answer away with it. The guide is a transient minimap of
the focused strip that appears above the cover, outlives it, and fades on its own clock — off by default, since a
window manager must not put a HUD on somebody's screen they did not ask for.

## Implementation

The thesis is that the guide is `Engine.emitLayerFrames`' projection at another scale, so almost none of it is new
geometry. The content is the *same expression* the cover blits from —
`workspaces.naturalFrames(scrollOffset:metrics:widths:)` at the live offset and the live widths, unmodified — which
is what makes off-screen columns, workspace switches sliding vertically, and animated resizes all appear in the
guide with none of them implemented a second time.

**What the guide shows is the strip, and `span` is a ceiling on that rather than a frame.** The obvious
construction — a window of `span` screens fixed on the viewport, with the strip sliding through it — is wrong, and
wrong in a way that only shows up once you use it: a frame fixed on the viewport can only ever show you the middle
of itself, so the marker never moves and walking to either end of the strip leaves a ribbon half empty on one side.
So the shown window is the **focused strip's own extent**, unioned with the viewport (an indicator wider than the
panel containing it is not an indicator), and capped at `span` screens only once the strip is longer than that. Two
things follow, and both are the point. The guide **shrinks** to what is actually on the strip — two screens of
windows draw a two-screens-wide ribbon. And it is the **viewport indicator that travels**: at either end of a long
strip the window pins to the content's edge and the indicator slides to that end, while in the middle it centres
and the tiles slide past it instead. The regimes meet continuously, being a `min` inside a `max` rather than a
branch, so nothing jumps as a strip grows past the cap. The extent is the focused strip's and not every
workspace's: a long strip somewhere you cannot see would otherwise size the guide for one you can.

That the guide's width tracks the strip's is also why the ribbon is a **layer inside a fixed window** rather than
the window's own frame. An animated resize changes the strip's extent once per frame; a window resizing at 120 Hz
spends a window-server round trip and a backing-store reallocation on each one, where a layer frame is an
assignment inside the `CATransaction` the blit already opens.

The one thing the core genuinely gains is the focus ring's travel, because no existing quantity can carry it. It is
`RectAnimator` used exactly as `windowAnimators` uses it, and for the identical reason: focus moves between two
*different* windows, so there is no shared number to interpolate and the destination must stay derived. What is new
is that nothing derives *from* it, and three exclusions follow. It is not in `Motion.isSettled`, which is the
transition's close gate — a ring still travelling would hold the cover up, a decoration blocking a cross-fade. It
never bumps `retargetGeneration`, which arms `Runtime.syncHold` — a focus change must not be able to extend a hung
transition's deadline. And it survives `closeTransition()`, which drops the widths and the displacements. That in
turn forces a second clock question: `Motion.needsFrames` is `isTransitioning || !isFocusRingSettled`, `syncClock`
reads it, and `syncHold` deliberately keeps reading the first term alone. With `style = "off"` no ring is ever
created, so the gate is byte-identical to the one it replaced.

Seeding the ring is done at the **tail of `reduce`**, which is the one decision here worth arguing. Focus reaches
`World.setFocus` from a dozen paths — a command, a system event, a workspace switch, a departure's refocus, an
arrival, boot — and the ring's travel is a *difference between two states* that no single one of those paths can
see. Touching all twelve would have been twelve chances to forget one; instead the switch was renamed `fold` and
`reduce` wraps it with `trackFocusRing`, which compares the two focused windows first and reads two `naturalFrames`
only when they differ. Both reads are taken at `scrollOffset: 0`, so the scroll cancels out of the delta — the same
two-reads-one-offset rule `finishStructuralEdit` runs on.

Everything else is `EmiraShell`, split the way `MenuBar` splits: `GuideModel` is the arithmetic (the projection,
the nine anchors, the diffed trigger, the rounding and padding rules) with no AppKit in it and a test file of its
own; `GuidePanel` is a sibling of `Overlay` and copies most of it because it is already right (`animationBehavior =
.none`, ordered in permanently at alpha 0 so a show is a pure alpha flip, `ignoresMouseEvents`,
`NSAnimationContext` fades). Two deliberate departures from the sibling. It sits one level above `.floating`, so
the guide is over the cover rather than under it. And it goes up at **full alpha**, not the `0.999` the cover stops
at: that thousandth exists to stop a full-alpha window marking what is behind it occluded, and a translucent panel
that covers nothing whole can neither do that nor need to avoid it.

`GuideSettings.scale` clamps `k = min(width, 1)/span` at 1 so the ribbon can never be bigger than one working area,
which is the whole of the render-time clamp the config surface leaves it — `Setting.Bound` has floors and no
ceilings, so an over-wide `width` or an under-one `span` (which parses, `span` being merely `> 0`, and would
otherwise make a panel taller than the screen) is stopped by the geometry rather than refused by the parser.
`width` is a fraction only, deliberately not `sizeList`'s dual "≤ 1 is a fraction, more is points" unit: a scalar
version of that would need a `Kind` case serving exactly one key, which the schema's own note names as the sign the
table has stopped paying for itself.

Separation between tiles is a **paint-time inset in guide points**, applied after projection, so it is constant
whatever `k` is and whatever the user's gaps are. `column-gap` defaults to 0, which would otherwise draw the
minimap as one undifferentiated bar; re-deriving the frames with a second set of gaps would have made a second
geometry authority out of a cosmetic problem. It is never more than a quarter of an extent, so a tile at a high
`span` thins rather than inverting.

**A tile carries no edge of its own, because a division is drawn once, on the boundary, and runs the whole length
of what it divides.** Bordering each tile draws every division twice and leaves a heap of chips where there should
be a picture of a strip. So `GuideModel.boundaries(of:frames:)` emits the midpoint of every gap between adjacent
columns and every gap between windows stacked in one — the midpoint rather than either edge, so `column-gap = 0`
puts the line exactly on the shared edge and a wide gap centres it in the space. The length is the load-bearing
part and the reason a gap alone would not have done: a gap between two stacked windows and a gap between two
columns look identical, where a rule running the full height of the columns it parts and one stopping at its own
column's edge do not. The set reads as the strip's structure rather than as a row of ticks. Two decisions in how
they are carried. A separator is emitted as a **degenerate `Rect`** — no width for a column boundary, no height for
a stack one — so it goes through `Projection.project` with everything else instead of needing a point projection of
its own, and the thickness stays in `GuidePanel` with the rest of the look, where the panel fattens the line about
its own boundary. And they are **plain layers pooled by count**, not the shape layers the tiles are: a boundary has
no identity to key a pool on, the count changes only when the strip's shape does, and a rectangle half a point wide
wants no path. They go in directly above the viewport, so they stay under the tiles however often the pool above
them is rebuilt.

The separation and the rule are not the same statement, which is why both are there. The gap is what makes a tile a
tile — a chip with its own rounded corners — and the rule is what says which kind of boundary the gap is. Flush
tiles divided only by rules is the other coherent design, and it is a different one: it wants the tile radius at
zero, since four rounded tiles meeting at a point leave a notch of panel fill at the junction.

**A corner conforms concentrically.** The ribbon's curve is generous — 20 pt, held to a quarter of its short side,
so that at a small `width` the guide reads as a rounded rectangle and not as a lozenge — and a tile pushed into the
end of the strip would otherwise be sliced square by the ribbon's own clip.
`GuideModel.corners(of:in:radius:outer:)` gives a rect its own radius everywhere except near a corner of its
container, where a corner `d` points inside the container's is drawn at `outer − d`, the one curve that holds a
constant gap from it. Three properties are the point. It decays *linearly* with the approach and expires exactly
where it meets the rect's own radius, so a tile scrolling off the end of the ribbon gives the curve up continuously
and nothing pops. `d` is the **larger** of the two offsets, so a tile flush with the top edge but well along it is
against an edge, not in a corner, and gets no curve it would have to explain. And the offsets floor at zero: a tile
hanging off the ribbon has its corner outside the clip, where the ribbon's own curve is already what the eye sees,
and letting the offset go negative would hand a tile scrolled half off the end a pill for a corner. Every radius is
capped at half the short side, below which a rounded rect stops being one.

It is measured against the border's **inner** edge — one point in, one point tighter — because that is the line a
tile is actually held off. The ring and the viewport go through the same call, which is what makes a focused corner
tile, its ring and the ribbon three concentric curves rather than three unrelated ones. The rule lives in
`GuideModel` with the separation, for the separation's reason: it is arithmetic and the panel is wiring. The radii
themselves stay in `GuidePanel`'s `// MARK: - The look`, so both functions take them rather than knowing them.

**Four radii need a path**, since `CALayer.cornerRadius` is one number for all four, so every rounded thing
*inside* the ribbon is a `RoundedLayer`. One path serves both fill and stroke because a `CAShapeLayer` has only
one: it is the silhouette inset by half the line width, so the stroke straddles the silhouette's edge, covers the
outer half the fill does not reach, and ends up inside the bounds exactly where a `borderWidth` would have drawn
it. A mask is minted only for a `preview`: a still fills its tile and has to be cut to the corners, where an
inscribed icon is padded away from them and never reaches one — so the default style pays no offscreen pass per
tile. The path is rebuilt only when the size or the corners change, and a scroll changes neither for any tile but
the ones at the ends. The ribbon itself keeps its `cornerRadius`: its four corners are always equal, and
`masksToBounds` is what clips the tiles running off its ends, so the path machinery would buy it nothing.

**A placeholder icon's padding tracks its tile's squareness.** An icon is square and a tile is not, and
`contentsGravity = .resizeAspect` has exactly one behaviour: it hands the icon the tile's whole short side, which
in a tile anywhere near square is an icon at full height, reading as a button rather than as a hint of what the
window is. So `GuideModel.placeholder(in:)` states the size outright: three fifths of the short side in a square
tile, 85% of it at 2:1 or thinner, linear between, centred. The parameter is the *aspect* and not an axis — a
narrow column and a window stacked in one are the same tile turned sideways, and both want their padding back,
because by the time the short side is half the long one the padding costs more than it buys. A constant inset was
the obvious alternative and it is wrong at both ends: tiles span an order of magnitude in size across `span`'s
range, so a number that leaves a big tile's icon alone eats a small one whole. The three numbers are one tuple,
`GuideModel.icon`, because they are one rule and the two anchors were tuned by eye. Expressing it needs a layer the
icon can be *placed* in, since a gravity cannot carry an inset; the alternative was `contentsRect` outside the unit
square, which is not padding — it replicates the image's edge pixels rather than adding transparent space, so an
icon whose border is not already clear would smear rather than shrink.

`preview` costs zero captures — it draws the stills a cover already leaves behind. `CaptureService.keep` was gated
on `mode == .immediate`; it is now a settable `keepsStills`, because two features want those stills and neither is
the other's, and the daemon names the union in one place. `SurfaceCache` gained `anySurface(for:)`, which reads a
still **size-agnostically**; `surface(for:at:)` keeps its size match untouched, because that check is load-bearing
for `CoverMode.immediate`, where a stale still stands in at full size and must not pass for the window. At a few
percent of scale the trade reverses: staleness is invisible and a hole is not. A window nothing has filmed falls
back to the icon placeholder, per tile.

Adding `Config.guide` tripped exactly the two tests it should have, and reading the diff was the point of both: the
`Mirror`-based coverage test demanded a config story for the new field, and `emira.example.toml` had to be
regenerated. The manual capturer's stills were 1×1 pixels, which is why `keep`'s gate had no coverage at all —
nothing it was ever handed could survive `SurfaceCache.reduced`. It gained a `pixels:` knob (defaulting to the old
behaviour) so the gate could be pinned in both directions.

## Observations

Three things about when the guide appears are deliberately left to the eye, and all three are one-line changes if
they read badly.

**The trigger does not include a resize.** It is `{ focused window, workspace, ordered column ids + their window
ids, scroll target }`, so `cycle-width`, `grow` and `fullscreen` change nothing in it: a resize while the guide is
already up keeps it up and animates correctly, but a resize alone does not summon it. Defensible — the guide is
about *where you are on the strip* — but it is the one command that visibly changes the strip's shape without
showing you the change.

**The dwell re-arms on every tick.** "Fade `duration` after everything stops" is what re-arming on `needsFrames`
buys, at the price of one inert scheduled closure per frame of motion (`DelayScheduler` has no cancellation, so the
generation-token idiom is the only one available without a new seam). Arming once at the trigger instead is a
one-line change; a debounce that keeps the semantics and schedules O(1) closures would need a wall clock the guide
does not currently own.

**The boot scan summons it.** The guide is primed before `onStateChanged` is wired, so it does not react to its own
birth — but adopting the desktop *is* a real rearrangement, so with the guide on it appears for `duration` at
login.

**Only the focused strip is divided.** The boundaries come from `state.layout.columns`, like the extent does, so a
workspace sliding vertically through the panel brings its rules with it — during the switch that is already the
strip being switched *to* — while the one sliding *out* has none. The tiles are there and correct throughout; it is
the lines over them that appear at the start of the switch rather than travelling in from the old arrangement.
Drawing both would mean boundaries for every materialized workspace, which is a second reading of the layout for
the half second one of them is visible.

The clamp at zero costs strict concentricity in one place. The ring and the viewport are drawn on the ribbon's
outer edge, a point outside the inner rect the offsets are measured against, so both take `outer` where a point
more would be exactly concentric with the ribbon's outer curve. It is a sub-point difference in a ~19 pt radius
against a 1 pt border, and the fix — letting the offset go negative — is the thing that gives an overhanging tile a
pill end.

A tile's radius only ever grows toward the ribbon's; nothing shrinks it below the 4 pt it starts at. That is right
for the corner case and untested against the one where a tile is *smaller* than the curve it is approaching, since
the cap at half the short side takes over there and the tile becomes a lozenge inside the ribbon's corner. It reads
as fitting the corner rather than as a mistake, but it is the shape most likely to need a second look at a high
`span`.

Both anchors of the padding rule — three fifths and 85% — and the 2:1 knee between them are eye-tuned. They are one
tuple in one place precisely because they will want moving once the guide has been lived with at a few `span`s. The
separator's hairline is the same kind of number: half a point, one device pixel at 2×, and deliberately lighter
than the ribbon's own 1 pt edge, because a grid of interior rules as heavy as the frame around them is a cage.

**A very short strip draws a very small guide**, since the shrink is linear: one screen of content at the default
`width = 0.2, span = 3` is a ribbon a fifteenth of the working width. That is the honest reading of "shrink to
fit", but a floor — or a non-linear one — is the obvious next knob if it reads as too small to be worth glancing
at.

Untested by construction: `Guide`, `GuidePanel`, `GuideIcons` and `RoundedLayer`, which need a window server, and
the `keepsStills` union in `applyShellConfig`, which lives in an executable. The arithmetic carries the assertions
— 41 in `GuideModelTests` covering the projection, the anchors, the trigger, the separators, the corner decay and
its expiry, the caps and the overhang, and the padding at both anchors and monotone between them; 12 more on the
ring in `GuideRingTests`.

Not addressed: the guide lives on `screens[0]`, the same display the overlay and the strip use, so it inherits open
item 4 (per-monitor strips) rather than adding to it. A workspace switch resolves both frames at their own focused
positions, so the ring's delta comes out purely horizontal — it glides across rather than following the strip a
screen up, which may or may not read correctly. And the legibility floor at high `span` is real arithmetic rather
than a guess: a tile is `(width/span) × workingHeight` tall, so a `width` of 0.05 over twenty screens is about 2
pt.
