# The presentation fence

> Change `1785571170` — 2026-08-01

## Goal

Focus changes occasionally showed a one-frame gap between windows: the transition itself was perfect and the reveal
was perfect, so whatever it was had to be happening in the instant the cover went up. It was. The reducer emitted
`beginTransition` and the teleports in one batch, and `CompositingExecutor` ran the presentation half and then
handed the truth half to `AXExecutor` microseconds later. An app quick enough to answer its AX set inside the
interval between the raise being *made* and the cover being *shown* moved where the user could see it, leaving a
window-sized hole for one frame — after which every other window closed the gap and the rest of the transition was
correct, which is exactly why it read as intermittent rather than as a bug in the geometry.

## Implementation

The fix is a third session phase, `.capturing → .raising → .covered`, and a new `Event.coverOnScreen` that
advances the last step. `beginTransition` and the cover's first blits go out on the last `captureReady` as before;
nothing on the truth plane goes with them. The shell fences the raise against the display and reports back, and
that report is the only thing that teleports.

**Emitting the raise first was already true and was never the property that mattered.** `runs(of:)` preserves
emission order, so the raise did reach the window server before the teleport was dispatched. What it cannot supply
is a guarantee about the *display*: a cover carries a full-screen base plus a still per window, an app's answer is
a geometry change on a surface that already exists, and the cheaper of the two can be composed first. Committing
first only orders our two calls, and the race is between our pixels and theirs. That is also why the fix belongs in
the core rather than in a deferral inside `CompositingExecutor`: an executor that buffered the truth run across
run-loop turns would have to buffer every subsequent truth effect too, or reorder a later batch ahead of the held
one — and it would move a sequencing decision out of the reducer, which is where the rest of the transition
lifecycle is decided.

**`Overlay.raise(onScreen:)` is the fence, and it asks the display rather than Core Animation.** Measured, so that
nobody re-runs the search: a `CATransaction` completion block on an animation-free transaction fires in 0.35–0.75
ms — it acknowledges the commit, not the compose — and `NSWindow.alphaValue` through a zero-duration animator is
applied synchronously, so neither says anything about what is on screen. `CADisplayLink.targetTimestamp` does: it
is when the frame being composed at this callback will be shown, and a commit that happened before the callback is
in that frame or an earlier one. So the fence latches that timestamp at the first callback after the raise and
fires at the first callback past it — two callbacks, measured at 12–16 ms on a 120 Hz panel.

That cost is paid where nothing can see it. The cover is up and its first frames are pixel-identical to the desktop
they replace, so nothing on screen is early or late; only the AX writes wait. Under `smooth` a spring runs for
hundreds of milliseconds afterwards and absorbs it whole. Under `snap`, where the close waits on the landings
alone, it is 15 ms added to an AX round trip already longer than that. It has its own `CADisplayLink` rather than
the pump's `DisplayLinkDriver`, which is gated on `Motion.needsFrames` and owned by the event loop: both are paused
between transitions, and coupling a presentation fence to the clock that advances the springs would put one
subsystem's cadence inside another's.

**`.raising` answers the truth plane exactly as `.capturing` does**, which is the half of the change that closes
the hole rather than moving it. `reassertTruthPlane` writes nothing in either, so a mouse-up, a display change or
an observed frame change landing inside the fence cannot write to a desktop that is still what the eye is looking
at; the teleport `coverOnScreen` carries reads whatever they would have written, which is the deferral
`.capturing` already ran on, extended by one phase. Ticks are inert for the same reason (`isCovered` is
`.covered` alone), so no spring advances inside the fence and motion starts after the reals have been told where to
go. That leaves the cover's first on-screen frame exactly what the raise blitted, which is the capture-time geometry
under `smooth` and the finished strip under `snap`, where `aimViewport` has already moved `viewportOffset.current`.

What does *not* wait is the layer tree, and `Motion.hasLayers` is where the two questions come apart. A retarget
during the fence sweeps in windows the session was not scoped for, and each owes a layer from the moment the tree
exists — `isReadyToExtend` and `extendCover` gate on `hasLayers`, not `isCovered`, because no later event would
come back for a window whose `captureReady` had already been spent. `coverUnavailable` moved the other way, from
`!isCovered` to `phase == .capturing`: a cover on its way to the glass is a cover, and abandoning it is the
cross-fade's job.

`coverOnScreen` is gated on the phase *advancing*, not on being covered. A stale fence — one belonging to a cover
this session replaced — would otherwise re-run the initial teleport, and the initial teleport is the one that
*replaces* the landing wait rather than adding to it, which would free sets still in flight.

`MockExecutor` acks `beginTransition` with `coverOnScreen` immediately, as an infinitely fast system's cover would.
The test helpers that stand in for the daemon do the same (`EngineTests.settle`, `drive`, `Settle.swift`,
`InstantCover`), and `LatentWorld` — the frame-stepped instrument that models capture and AX latency — gained a
`coverLatency` of one frame, the only latency in it that is a display fact rather than an IPC.

## Observations

The fence is a strong guarantee about the *raise's own frame* and not a proof that the render server has finished
rasterizing a large base image. There is no public API that would give the second; if a gap is ever seen again it
would be the place to look, and the answer would be a third callback rather than a different mechanism.

The two-callback rule follows the display's cadence, so on a panel that has dropped to a low refresh rate the fence
is proportionally longer — 33 ms at 60 Hz, more if ProMotion idles further down. A transition is active motion, so
the display should be at its native cadence throughout, but nothing here enforces that.

`Overlay` became an `NSObject` to be a `CADisplayLink` target, as `DisplayLinkDriver` already is. A private proxy
object was the alternative and buys nothing at one call site.

Untested by construction: the fence itself, which needs a window server. What the suite covers is the contract
around it — that the raise batch carries no truth-plane effect, that nothing else may write during `.raising`, that
the teleport rides the report, and that `CompositingExecutor` acks only when the surface says so. The timings
quoted above were measured with standalone harnesses against the real display, not derived.
