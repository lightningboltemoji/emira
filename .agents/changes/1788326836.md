# A hoist comes down when the window is actually there

> Change `1788326836` — 2026-09-01

## Goal

Clicking a hoisted float flashed: the picture was cut away and the tiled window that had been covering it
showed for a frame or two before the real float arrived in front.

`1788325925` left the seam open and its own observations named the risk. The core drops a hoist when
`Event.focusChanged` says the float is in front — and that is an **AX notification**. It says the app told
us its focus moved, not that the window server has published the raise. `HoistPanels.apply` called
`retire()` → `orderOut` synchronously on the report, so nothing anywhere waited for the thing the picture
was standing in for to actually be standing there.

## Implementation

**A released panel stops taking clicks and keeps its pixels.** Those are two different moments and the
flash was in treating them as one. The click that caused the release has already been spent, so the panel
gives up the mouse immediately and anything after it belongs to the real window coming forward; the
picture stays until that window is genuinely in front of it.

**`StackProbe` is the question, and only the window server can answer it.**
`CGWindowListCopyWindowInfo(.optionOnScreenAboveWindow)` is everything being drawn in front of the float,
narrowed to `kCGWindowLayer == 0` and to windows that are not ours — the hoist's own panel, the cover and
the guides are all above it by construction. Re-asked rather than awaited, because there is no
notification for "the raise reached the glass", and asked off the main thread for
`Overlay.confirmPublished`'s reason: what is being waited out is another app's activation and its length
is not ours to block for. Unreadable is not "still covered" — the answer is gone rather than negative,
and a picture held up forever on a failed read is worse than the frame it would have saved.

**The fence is a delay, not a veto.** `releaseGrace` bounds it and expiry dissolves anyway. The
alternative is tempting and wrong: if the window server still says the float is buried, the honest-looking
move is to keep the hoist up. But the core has dropped it, and a shell that keeps a panel the core does
not know about is exactly the second opinion about what is on the screen that `PRINCIPLES` §1 forbids. An
activation macOS refused leaves the float genuinely behind, and that is a focus failure with its own
answer (`AXFrontmost` where AppKit is refused), not something a picture should paper over.

**The release dissolves rather than cuts**, over `Reconstruction.refreshDuration`'s 0.12 s and for its
reason — this is the same event in the other direction, a stand-in becoming the window's own pixels. It is
not only about ordering: the still was filmed while the float was behind *and unfocused*, so it hands over
to a window whose title bar is styled differently and whose app may have redrawn underneath, which is the
absence the cover's own cross-fade already exists to carry.

**A float buried again mid-release reclaims its own panel** rather than building a second one over it, and
films nothing, since the pixels never left the screen. Every release and reclaim bumps a per-window
generation, so a fence answering late — or a dissolve completing after the float came back — owns nothing.
`Overlay.fadeOut`'s idiom, and `reclaim` writes its alpha through the animator at zero duration for
`Overlay.raise`'s reason: a bare assignment is overwritten by the next frame of the dissolve it is
cancelling.

**`HoistSurface` is new, and `HoistPanels` now builds through a factory.** The fence is policy with races
in it — three generations, a reclaim, a grace — and none of it is AppKit. `CoverSurface` is a protocol for
exactly this reason, so this follows it: `HoistPanel` is the real one, `HoistPlaneTests` drives the
policy against a recording surface, a fake probe and a manual scheduler. Ordering crosses the seam as an
`Int` handle (`NSWindow.windowNumber`, which is what `order(_:relativeTo:)` takes) rather than as another
surface, so the implementation needs no downcast.

`HoistPanel` also stopped being an `NSObject`: it has no selectors, and inheriting one collided its
`release()` with ObjC's.

## Observations

**The flash on the way *in* is untouched and is not fixable this way.** A float is genuinely buried for
the length of its film — one screenshot, ~25 ms and up — because there is nothing to draw until the first
photograph exists. Two routes would close it, both trading freshness for latency: show a `SurfaceCache`
still from a previous burial and cross-fade to the new film (`CoverMode.immediate`, applied to hoists),
which only helps from the second burial on and shows a reduced still first; or film speculatively when a
hoist is *released*, so the next burial has pixels waiting — fresher in time to display, staler in
content, and wearing an active title bar on a window that is no longer focused.

**The fence's cost is a window-server round trip per release**, on a queue of its own, and it is asked at
most every refresh until it clears. In the common case it clears on the first ask.

**Validated by the suite, not against the daemon**, on the same terms `1788325925` records: seeing it live
means `make install` over a running window manager and an Accessibility re-grant. `HoistPlaneTests` drives
the fence through the states that produced the defect, but the one thing it cannot answer is whether
`optionOnScreenAboveWindow` reports what this argument assumes at the moment an app activates. That is the
confirming pass worth making on the next install.

**`releaseGrace` is a guess, not a measurement.** 0.5 s, chosen because nothing waits on it — the panel it
holds up is a pixel-identical copy of the window coming forward, so overshooting costs a stale title bar
and undershooting costs the flash back. Whether the real gap is 5 ms or 200 ms is unmeasured; the fence
exits as soon as it clears, so the number only bounds the pathological case.
